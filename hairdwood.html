🪜 The Ladder

How the ladder works

One bet a day: a single player prop — points, rebounds, assists or threes made, whichever of the four grades highest on the whole slate — staked from a $100 account.

Win and the entire return rides the next day. Five straight closes the cycle and the money comes off the table, with the next cycle seeded at 10% of the grown account. Any miss ends the cycle there and the next one starts 25% bigger.

Only the cycle's seed is ever your money — everything above it is the book's — so a miss on day four costs exactly what a miss on day one costs. That is the one thing separating this from a martingale.

A prop is voided, not lost, if the man does not play: the stake comes back and the same rung runs again tomorrow. That is why a questionable tag costs grade points here instead of being multiplied into the probability.

The pick stays blank until it locks one hour before the first tip, then shows the rung actually published to the repo. Read the red panel on the card before you run it.

🎲 Same-game doubles

Why two legs from one game — and why the singles usually beat them

Every pair inside each game is enumerated and graded, not just the two likeliest: two props in one game share a pace, a game script and sometimes a basketball, and the direction differs for every pair of markets — two teammates chasing the same rebound take them off each other, while an assist and a teammate's three can be the same event.

That is the theory. The test did not find it. Graded against a season of real results, doubles landed almost exactly what you get by multiplying their two legs together — so the correlation that justifies a same-game parlay is, on the evidence so far, too small for this board to see. Every ρ is halved because of it, the published pair carries a further haircut for the flattery in picking the best two legs of a game, and the card still shows ρ and what it is worth so you can watch the claim rather than take it.

So read these as what they are. A parlay pays the book's margin twice and this one has no measured correlation edge to pay for that. The singles above are usually the better bet; these are here because a same-game double is what you asked the board for, priced as honestly as it can be rather than sold. Cards are ordered by the pair's grade, on the same hundred-point scale as everything else.
