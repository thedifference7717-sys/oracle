🪜 The Ladder

How the ladder works

One bet a day: a single “to record a hit” leg — the highest-edge hitter on the whole slate — staked from an account you set yourself. Every figure on the card is worked out from your number.

Win and the entire return rides the next day. Five straight closes the cycle and the money comes off the table, with the next cycle seeded at 10% of the grown account. Any miss ends the cycle there and the next one starts 25% bigger.

Only the cycle's seed is ever your money — everything above it is the book's — so a miss on day four costs exactly what a miss on day one costs. That is the one thing separating this from a martingale.

The pick stays blank until it locks one hour before the day's first pitch, then shows the rung actually published to the repo, with its publish time beside first pitch. Read the red panel on the card before you run it.

🎲 Bet cards

Why these games and no others

A card for every game whose opposing arm is soft enough, and nothing else — the same rule the Telegram alerts use, so the board and your phone cannot disagree about what counts as a bet.

Soft arm holds the offence at league average and asks only how bad the opposing starter, his bullpen, the defence and the park are, in points of hit probability against a league-neutral leg. It rates the spot rather than the hitters, which is why it decides eligibility: an ace is a different bet from batting practice even when the price says otherwise.

The price no longer gates anything. Each card still shows the fair price, your price and the edge between them, because whether a qualifying spot is worth taking at the number in front of you is a real question — it is just a different question from whether the spot qualifies. A soft spot at a bad price is a reason to shop for a better price, not a reason to hide the game.

The likeliest double is usually the most heavily bet and the worst priced. What pays is the gap between our number and the book's, which is why these are ranked by edge rather than by probability.
