🏈 The doubles

Why these games and no others

Two props in one game, and the book's arithmetic on them is wrong. A parlay pays the product of its legs' decimal odds, which prices them as if they were independent. Two men in one football game are nothing of the sort: a quarterback's passing yards are his receivers' yards added up, two backs share one pile of carries, and the side that gets ahead stops throwing.

Every pair below is priced off ten thousand simulated afternoons instead, so the joint probability is the real one. A card is issued for every game whose best pair clears the bet bar, and for nothing below it.

The headline is edge points — our probability minus what the price needs — and the stake is that number times your dollars-per-point, so the sum on the card checks out by hand.

🎯 Single player props not loaded
Props are the softest market in football. This ranks every projection we make against a real price — the exchange's, if it is loaded, or your own book's if you paste the lines in below. Ranked by stake, after fees, and only where the edge clears the cost of actually getting the bet on.
Paste your book's prop lines, one per line — player, market, line, price. Anything readable works: "Drake Maye pass yds 249.5 -115", "A.J. Brown rec yds o48.5 -110 u+105", "Kupp receptions 3.5 -130"
Low · number · high is the middle half of ten thousand simulated games — the 25th, the median and the 75th, with the 10th and 90th dimmed underneath. A receiver whose number is 54 yards lands outside the low-to-high band a quarter of the time, and that is not the model being vague; that is what a football game is. Spot is how far this particular afternoon sits from a neutral one in his own units; hover it to see which of the four levers moved it — game script, the defence opposite, who is out, and weather.
A prop is only called a play when the edge clears the cost of getting it on — half the bid/ask spread plus the fee on the exchange, or the vig at your book. Our number is blended half-and-half with the market's first: the standard deviations agree with the exchange's to a fraction of a yard now, but a five-point disagreement in the middle remains and nothing here can prove whose it is.

🔥 The softest spots not priced
Not priced yet.
Three readings of the same afternoon, each in percent of what a league-average receiver does against nobody in particular. Soft defence holds the offence at league average and asks only how bad the men opposite are. Good offence does the reverse. Spot is the two together: what a typical pass-catcher in this offence actually does today. Best defence to worst is worth about fifty points on this slate, so the spot is usually a bigger lever than which receiver you pick out of it — the same thing the baseball board finds with pitching.

⚖️ Book vs exchange not loaded
A sportsbook keeps about 4.5% of a two-way market. Kalshi is an exchange: the two sides of an NFL moneyline quote a cent apart with six figures of size behind them, and it hangs a contract on every half point. Loading it lets the board buy the same outcome at whichever venue is actually cheaper — which needs no model to be right, and is the most reliable money on this page.
Every exchange price here is quoted all-in: the ask plus the trading fee, which is 7% × price × (1−price) — 1.75 cents on a coin flip, so an edge under two cents is not an edge. Kalshi serves its data without cross-origin headers, so a browser needs a bridge: deploy kalshi-proxy/worker.js (it is read-only, holds no credentials and cannot trade) and paste its URL into the bet slip below.

🔬 Does any of this actually win? walk-forward backtest
Rebuilds the ratings week by week from only what was known at the time, prices every game against the closing line the book actually hung, and grades it against the score that actually happened. Takes about a minute.
A backtest without an error bar is a story. One season is a few hundred bets and the standard error on that is several percent, so anything inside two standard errors of zero is noise no matter how good the ROI looks — this panel says so out loud rather than letting you talk yourself into it.
